\title{A multivariate extension of size-biased mixtures and its estimation using a penalized EM algorithm} \vskip 2.0cm
\author{Taehan Bae\footnote{Mathematics and Statistics, University of Regina, Regina,
Saskatchewan, Canada, taehan.bae@uregina.ca} and Himchan Jeong \footnote{Statistics and Actuarial Science, Simon Fraser University, Burnaby, British Columbia, Canada}}


\maketitle
\vskip 1.5cm

\begin{abstract}
Finite mixtures have been among the most widely used approaches for modeling complex data drawn from a population composed of heterogeneous sub-populations. In particular for insurance loss modelling, several mixtures of weighted distributions such as Erlang mixture, size-biased Weibull mixture and size-biased truncated lognormal mixture, have gained popularity due to the weak-denseness property.    
This paper aims to extend univariate size-biased mixture models to multivariate cases. A penalized EM algorithm that facilitates the purging of statistically insignificant components, and thus results in a parsimonious model than the one fitted by the classical EM algorithm, is employed for model estimation. Three specific multivariate size-biased models: Erlang, size-biased Weibull and size-biased truncated lognormal models, are considered for model fitting on a real dataset.      
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Finite mixture models (FMMs) have become a cornerstone of statistical modeling, providing a robust framework for analyzing data originating from heterogeneous populations. The foundational work of Titterington et al. (1985)\nocite{Titterington1985} established the strong theoretical underpinnings of FMMs and their wide-ranging applicability across diverse fields. Lindsay (1995) \nocite{Lindsay1995} further contributed to the understanding of FMMs, offering a comprehensive overview of their theory and applications, and highlighting their versatility in capturing complex data structures.

The appeal of FMMs lies in their ability to decompose a complex dataset into a mixture of simpler, more manageable components. By assuming that data points are drawn from a combination of underlying distributions, FMMs can effectively capture the heterogeneity present in the data and reveal the hidden population structures. This makes them particularly useful in situations where data points may belong to different subgroups, each with its own distinct characteristics.

In insurance loss modeling, the use of univariate FMMs has gained considerable traction, as demonstrated by the work of Lee and Lin (2010)\nocite{leelin2010} and Verbelen et al. (2015)\nocite{Verbelen2015}. Their research showcases the effectiveness of Erlang mixtures in modeling claim data, capturing the skewness and heavy tails often observed in insurance loss distributions. These characteristics are critical for accurately assessing risk and pricing insurance policies. In Blostein and Miljkovic (2019)\nocite{blostein2019modeling}, various finite mixtures with components from different parametric distribution families such as gamma, lognormal, Weibull and Burr distributions have been used for modelling left-truncated loss data.       

The work of Bae and Ko (2020)\nocite{bae2020mixtures} further underscores the flexibility of size-biased Weibull mixtures in modeling insurance loss data. Their findings reveal that size-biased Weibull mixtures outperform traditional models in capturing the tail behavior of insurance loss distributions, providing a more accurate assessment of large losses.  This is particularly important for measuring the tail risks associated with insurance losses.

Building on this foundation, Bae and Miljkovic (2024)\nocite{bae2024loss} introduce the size-biased truncated lognormal mixture, specifically tailored for left-truncated loss data. Left truncation occurs when data below a certain threshold is not observed, which is common in insurance settings where small claims may not be reported. The size-biased truncated lognormal mixture provides a more accurate representation of the loss distribution in such cases, leading to improved risk assessment and pricing.

While univariate FMMs have provided valuable insights, the extension to multivariate models has gained increasing momentum in recent years. Many real-world phenomena are characterized by multiple correlated variables, and capturing the relationships between these variables is essential for accurate modeling and prediction. Various multivariate mixture models beyond the Gaussian mixture have been investigated in Bouguila and Fan (2020)\nocite{Bouguila2020}. Their work highlights the potential of multivariate mixture models to uncover complex relationships between variables and to provide a more nuanced understanding of the underlying data structure. In actuarial domain, Lee and Lin (2012)\nocite{leelin2012} introduce a class of multivariate Erlang mixtures which extends the univariate Erlang mixture to higher dimensions. 

Parameter estimation in mixture models often relies on the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm (Dempster et al., 1977\nocite{dempster1977maximum}). However, EM can lead to overfitting, resulting in spurious model components. Penalized EM algorithms address this by encouraging the removal of insignificant components, leading to more parsimonious models (Figueiredo and Jain, 2002\nocite{Figueiredo}). 

The present paper aims to develop a unified modelling framework for multivariate size-biased mixture model classes including the multivariate Erlang mixture class. We utilize a penalized EM algorithm to estimate parameters in our multivariate size-biased mixture model, carefully balancing model fit and complexity. By extending size-biased mixtures to the multivariate setting and employing penalized EM, we provide a valuable tool for complex data analysis, with particular relevance to insurance and related fields. 

This paper is structured as follows: Section \ref{sec2} reviews univariate size-biased mixtures; Section \ref{sec3} introduces the framework for multivariate size-biased mixtures and derive expressions for some  dependence measures; Section \ref{sec4} details the parameter estimation using the EM  and the PEM algorithms. An application with a real bivariate loss data is given in Section \ref{sec5} followed by a concluding remarks in Section \ref{sec6}.


\section{Univariate size-biased mixtures}\label{sec2}
Let a positive continuous random variable $X$ have an $M$-component mixture of size-biased (SB) distributions on a (parametric) baseline density function $h(x; \bm{\theta}),\, x >0$. Specifically the probability density function (pdf) of $X$ is expressed as   
\beq\label{pdf_marginal}
f(x; \bm{\a}, \bm{\theta}) = \sum_{l = 1}^{M} \a_{l} g(x;s_l, \bm{\theta}),\quad x >0,
\eeq
where $\a_{l} \ge 0$ and $\sum_{l = 1}^{M} \a_{l} = 1$, and
\beq\label{sb_den}
g(x;s_l, \bm{\theta}) = \frac{x^{s_l -1} h(x; \bm{\theta})}{\mu(s_l -1; \bm{\theta})}.
\eeq
Here the SB orders $s_1 < s_2 < \cdots < s_{M}$ are positive integers (i.e., $s_l \in Z_+$), and $\mu(m; \bm{\theta}) := \int_0^{\infty} x^m h(x;\bm{\theta}) dx$ is the $m$-th raw moment of a random variable having the pdf $h$. Note that the parameter (vector) $\bm{\theta}$ is assumed to be common to all mixture components of $X$ for simplicity. 

Three specific examples of size-biased mixture class available in the literature are (i) Erlang mixtures (Lee and Lin, 2010\nocite{leelin2010}; Willmot and Lin, 2011\nocite{willmot2011risk}; Verbelen et al., 2015\nocite{reynkens2017modelling}) that are built on the exponential baseline density function $h(x;\theta) = \theta^{-1}\exp\{-x/\theta\}$, (ii) size-biased Weibull mixtures (Bae and Ko, 2020) constructed on the Weibull baseline density $h(x;\tau,\theta) = \tau \theta^{-\tau} x^{\tau-1}\exp\{-(x/\theta)^{\tau}\}$, and  (iii) size-biased left-truncated lognormal mixtures (Bae and Miljkovic, 2024\nocite{bae2024loss}) built on the left-truncated lognormal baseline density with a fixed left truncation threshold $d >0$ and the model parameter $\sigma >0$. The parameter $\mu$ in the lognormal distribution is set as $\mu = \log d$ for simplicity since it is unidentifiable in the presence of the SB orders in the size-biased components.  This simplification renders the following the baseline density for the size-biased left-truncated lognormal mixtures 
\bea
h(x;\sigma) = \frac{2}{x\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\left[\log(x/d)\right]^2\right\}.
\eea

The main theoretical characteristic of aforementioned size-based mixture models is the weak-denseness property. Roughly speaking, any positive continuous distribution can be approximated with either Erlang mixture or size-biased Weibull (SBW) mixture at an arbitrary precision as the number of components gets large. The same argument holds for the size-based left-truncated lognormal (SBtLN) mixture for any left truncated distributions. 

\section{Multivariate size-biased mixtures}\label{sec3}

Let $\bm{X} = (X_1,\ldots, X_d)$ be a $d$-dimensional random vector on $\bm{R}_+^d = (0,\infty)^d$ where the marginal distribution of $X_k$ is a finite mixture of size-biased random variables with (distinct) SB orders $(s_1^{(k)}, s_2^{(k)}, \ldots, s_{M_k}^{(k)})$ along with other parameter (vector) $\bm{\theta}_k$ passed down from the baseline density. For each $k = 1,\ldots, d$, let us define a discrete random variable $J_k$ which attains a value from the set of SB orders, $S_{J_k} := \{s_1^{(k)}, s_2^{(k)}, \ldots, s_{M_k}^{(k)}\}$.  

A $d$-dimensional random vector $\bm{J} = (J_1,\ldots, J_d)$ attains a grid point in $S_{\bm{J}} := \prod_{k=1}^d  S_{J_k}$ where the total number of grid points in $S_{\bm{J}}$ is $M := \prod_{k=1}^d M_k$.

We further denote the joint probability of $\bm{J}$ by
\bea
\a_{i_1,\ldots,i_d} := \mP\left(J_1 = s^{(1)}_{i_1}, \ldots, J_d = s^{(d)}_{i_d}\right) 
\eea
where $i_k \in \{1,\ldots, M_k\}$ for each $k = 1,\ldots, d$. 

Denoting $\bm{\Psi}$ the vector of all model parameters, we now write the joint density function of $\bm{X}$ as follows.
\beq\label{pdf_joint}
f(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_d; \bm{\Psi}) = \sum_{i_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{i_d = 1}^{M_d} \a_{i_1,i_2,\ldots,i_d} \prod_{k=1}^d g(x_k;s^{(k)}_{i_k}, \bm{\theta}_k). 
\eeq
Therefore the joint distribution of $\bm{X}$ is a finite mixture of $M$ multivariate (independent) distributions.   

Under this model specification, it is easy to see that the marginal distribution of $X_k$ is an $M_k$-point mixture of size-biased distributions, i.e., 
\beq\label{marginal_density}
f_k(x; \bm{\a}^{(k)}, \bm{\theta}_k) = \sum_{i_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{i_d = 1}^{M_d} \a_{i_1,\ldots,i_d}g(x;s^{(k)}_{i_k},\bm{\theta}_k) =  \sum_{l = 1}^{M_k} \a^{(k)}_{l} g(x;s^{(k)}_{l},\bm{\theta}_k),      
\eeq
where
\bea
\a^{(k)}_{l} =  \sum_{i_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots \sum_{i_{k-1} = 1}^{M_{k-1}}\sum_{i_{k+1} = 1}^{M_{k+1}}\cdots \sum_{i_d = 1}^{M_d} \a_{i_1,\ldots,i_{k-1}, l, i_{k+1},\ldots, i_d}.
\eea

The joint density function of a pair $(X_u, X_v)$ is expressed as follows.
\beq\label{pair_density}
f_{u,v}(x, y; \bm{\a}^{(u,v)}, \bm{\theta}_u, \bm{\theta}_v) = \sum_{l = 1}^{M_u}\sum_{m = 1}^{M_v} \a^{(u,v)}_{l,m} g(x;s^{(u)}_{l},\bm{\theta}_u)g(y;s^{(v)}_{m},\bm{\theta}_v),      
\eeq
where
\bea
\a^{(u,v)}_{l,m} =  \sum_{i_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots \sum_{i_{u-1} = 1}^{M_{u-1}}\sum_{i_{u+1} = 1}^{M_{u+1}}\cdots \sum_{i_{v-1} = 1}^{M_{v-1}}\sum_{i_{v+1} = 1}^{M_{v+1}}\cdots \sum_{i_d = 1}^{M_d} \a_{i_1,\ldots,i_{u-1}, l, i_{u+1},\ldots, i_{v-1}, m, i_{v+1},\ldots, i_d}.
\eea

By (\ref{sb_den}) and (\ref{pdf_joint}), it is easy to obtain the following expression for the joint moment of $\bm{X}$.
\beq\label{joint_mom}\nn
\mE\left[\prod_{k=1}^d X_{k}^{n_k}\right] &=& \int_0^{\infty} \cdots \int_0^{\infty}\left(\prod_{k=1}^d x_{k}^{n_k}\right) f(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_d; \bm{\Psi}) dx_1\ldots dx_d\\\nn
                               &=& \sum_{i_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{i_d = 1}^{M_d} \a_{i_1,i_2,\ldots,i_d} \prod_{k=1}^d \left[\int_0^{\infty} x_k^{n_k}g(x_k;s^{(k)}_{i_k}, \bm{\theta}_k) dx_k\right]\\
															 &=& \sum_{i_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{i_d = 1}^{M_d} \a_{i_1,i_2,\ldots,i_d} \prod_{k=1}^d \left[\frac{\mu(s^{(k)}_{i_k} + n_k -1;  \bm{\theta}_k)}{\mu(s^{(k)}_{i_k} -1;  \bm{\theta}_k)}\right].
\eeq


\subsection{Dependence measures}
Here we give explicit expressions of some commonly used dependence measures for the bivariate size-biased mixture distribution: covariance, Spearman’s rho and Kendall’s tau. 

\subsubsection{Covariance}

From the expression for the joint moment (\ref{joint_mom}), it is straightforward to obtain an expression for the covariance between two size-biased mixture random variables.

\begin{prop} Suppose $(X_u, X_v)$ has the bivariate size-biased mixture distribution with the joint density function $f_{u,v}(x, y; \bm{\a}^{(u,v)}, \bm{\theta}_u, \bm{\theta}_v)$ (\ref{pair_density}), and the marginal densities $f_u(x; \bm{\a}^{(u)}, \bm{\theta}_u)$ and $f_v(x; \bm{\a}^{(v)}, \bm{\theta}_v)$ (\ref{marginal_density}), respectively for $X_u$ and  $X_v$. Then the covariance is expressed as follows.
\beq
\mC[X_u, X_v] = \sum_{l = 1}^{M_u}\sum_{m = 1}^{M_v} \left(\a^{(u,v)}_{l,m} - \a^{(u)}_{l} \a^{(v)}_{m}\right)m(s_l^{(u)},\theta_u)m(s_m^{(v)},\theta_v),
\eeq
\end{prop}
where
\[
m(s,\theta)=\mathbb \int_0^\infty x g(x;s,\theta),dx 
:=\frac{\mu(s;\theta)}{\mu(s-1;\theta)}.
\]

\proof See Appendix.

\subsubsection{Spearman's rho}

The population version Spearman's rho ($\rho$) of random variables $T_1$ and $T_2$ with marginal densities $f_{T_1}(t_1)$ and $f_{T_2}(t_2)$, respectively, and the joint distribution $F(t_1, t_2)$ on $(0, \infty)\times (0, \infty)$, is defined as follows:
\beq\label{def:rho}\nn
\rho &=& \frac{\mE[U_1 U_2] - \mE[U_1]\mE[U_2]}{\sqrt{\mV[U_1]}\sqrt{\mV[U_2]}}\\
&=& 12 \int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty} F(t_1, t_2) f_{T_1}(t_1)f_{T_2}(t_2)\,dt_1 dt_2 -3,
\eeq
where $U_1 = F_{T_1}(T_1)$ and $U_2 = F_{T_2}(T_2)$ are uniform random variables. The following gives an expression for the Spearman's rho of a pair $(X_u, X_v)$ with the joint density function (\ref{pair_density}).
\begin{prop} Suppose $(X_u, X_v)$ has the bivariate size-biased mixture distribution with the joint density function $f_{u,v}(x, y; \bm{\a}^{(u,v)}, \bm{\theta}_u, \bm{\theta}_v)$ given in (\ref{pair_density}), and the marginal densities $f_u(x; \bm{\a}^{(u)}, \bm{\theta}_u)$ and $f_v(x; \bm{\a}^{(v)}, \bm{\theta}_v)$ given in (\ref{marginal_density}), respectively for $X_u$ and  $X_v$. Then the Spearman's rho is expressed as follows.
\beq\label{rho_exp}
\rho = 12\sum_{l = 1}^{M_u}\sum_{m = 1}^{M_v} \sum_{l^\prime = 1}^{M_u}\sum_{m^\prime = 1}^{M_v} \a^{(u,v)}_{l,m} \a^{(u)}_{l^\prime}\a^{(v)}_{m^\prime} \lambda(s_l, s_{l^\prime}, \bm{\theta}_u)\lambda(s_m, s_{m^\prime}, \bm{\theta}_v) -3, 
\eeq
where
\bea
\lambda(s, t, \bm{\theta}) := \int_0^{\infty} G(x;s, \bm{\theta})g(x; t, \bm{\theta})dx.
\eea
Here $G(x;s, \bm{\theta}) := \int_0^x g(y; s, \bm{\theta})dy$ is the distribution function of the size-based random variable with density (\ref{sb_den}).
\end{prop}

\proof See Appendix.

\subsubsection{Kendall's tau}

Kendall's tau ($\tau$) is often used as a measure of concordance between two random variables. Specifically, the population version Kendall's tau of continuous random variables $T_1$ and $T_2$ with marginal densities $f_{T_1}(t_1)$ and $f_{T_2}(t_2)$, respectively, and the joint distribution $F(t_1, t_2)$ on $(t_1, t_2) \in (0, \infty)\times (0, \infty)$, is defined as follows:
\beq\label{def:tau}\nn
\tau &=& \mP[(T_1-T_1^{\prime})(T_2 - T_2^{\prime}) > 0] - \mP[(T_1-T_1^{\prime})(T_2 - T_2^{\prime}) < 0]\\
&=& 4 \int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty} F(t_1, t_2) f(t_1, t_2)\,dt_1 dt_2 - 1,
\eeq
where $(T_1, T_2)$ and  $(T_1^{\prime}, T_2^{\prime})$ are independent and identically distributed random vectors each with the joint distribution $F$. Similarly as Spearman's rho, the following expression of Kendall's tau is available for a pair of size-biased mixture random variables.

\begin{prop} Suppose $(X_u, X_v)$ has the bivariate size-biased mixture distribution with the joint density function $f_{u,v}(x, y; \bm{\a}^{(u,v)}, \bm{\theta}_u, \bm{\theta}_v)$ given in (\ref{pair_density}), and the marginal densities $f_u(x; \bm{\a}^{(u)}, \bm{\theta}_u)$ and $f_v(x; \bm{\a}^{(v)}, \bm{\theta}_v)$ given in (\ref{marginal_density}), respectively for $X_u$ and  $X_v$. Then the Kendall's tau is expressed as follows.
\beq\label{tau_exp}
\tau = 4\sum_{l = 1}^{M_u}\sum_{m = 1}^{M_v} \sum_{l^\prime = 1}^{M_u}\sum_{m^\prime = 1}^{M_v} \a^{(u,v)}_{l,m} \a^{(u,v)}_{l^{\prime, m^{\prime}}} \lambda(s_l, s_{l^\prime}, \bm{\theta}_u)\lambda(s_m, s_{m^\prime}, \bm{\theta}_v) -1, 
\eeq
where
\bea
\lambda(s, t, \bm{\theta}) = \int_0^{\infty} G(x;s, \bm{\theta})g(x; t, \bm{\theta})dx.
\eea
\end{prop}

\proof See Appendix.

Note that the expression $\lambda(s, t, \bm{\theta})$ involved in both (\ref{rho_exp}) and (\ref{tau_exp}) does not admit an explicit expression in general. For the Erlang mixture case where the baseline density is exponential, however, we have 
\bea
g(x; t, \theta) &=& \frac{x^{t-1}\exp[-x/\theta]}{\theta^t (t-1)!},\\
G(x; s, \theta) &=& 1- \sum_{j=0}^{s-1}\frac{x^j \exp[-x/\theta]}{\theta^j j!}.
\eea
Using these, it is easy to obtain the following explicit expression for $\lambda(s, t, \bm{\theta})$:

\beq
\lambda(s, t, \theta) = 1-  \sum_{j=0}^{s-1}\binom{j+t-1}{j}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{j+t}.
\eeq


\subsubsection{Upper tail dependence} 
Two random variables $X_u$ and $X_v$ having the bivariate size-biased mixture distribution are upper tail independent.  That is,
\bea
\lambda_U &=& \lim_{q\rightarrow 1}\mP\left[X_u > F^{-1}_u(q)\big{|}X_v > F^{-1}_v(q)\right]\\
          &=& \lim_{q\rightarrow 1}\frac{1- \mP[X_u \le F^{-1}_u(q)] - \mP[X_v \le F^{-1}_v(q)] + \mP[X_u \le F^{-1}_u(q), X_v \le F^{-1}_v(q)]}{1-q}\\
					&=& \lim_{q\rightarrow 1}\frac{1-2q + F_{u,v}(F^{-1}_u(q),F^{-1}_v(q))}{1-q} = 0,	 
\eea
where $F_{u,v}(x,y)$ is the bivariate joint distribution function given in (\ref{pair_density}). It is elementary show the above result using the ordering of the size-biased components. See Appendix for details. 


\section{Parameter estimation}\label{sec4}
\subsection{EM algorithm}\label{em}
The Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm (Dempster et al. 1977 \nocite{dempster1977maximum}) is widely used for the maximum likelihood estimation of finite mixture models in which each observation is generated from a randomly selected distribution out of a set of distributions with (prior) selection probabilities.   
 
Let $\bm{x} := \{\bm{x}_1, \ldots, \bm{x}_n\}$ be a set of independent observations drawn from the joint distribution with the pdf (\ref{pdf_joint}). The vector of model parameters, $\bm{\Psi} = (\bm{\a}, \bm{\Theta})$, is composed of the vector of mixture weight parameters $\bm{\a}$ and all parameters involved in the baseline density functions $\bm{\Theta} = (\bm{\theta}_1,\ldots, \bm{\theta}_d)$. Denote $Z_{i,\bm{j}} : = Z_{i, (j_1,\ldots,j_d)}$ as the membership indicator random variable that takes the value 1 if the $i$th observation $\bm{x}_i = (x_{i1},\ldots,x_{id})$ is generated from the component with the vector of SB orders $(s^{(1)}_{j_1},\ldots, s^{(d)}_{j_d}) \in S_J$, zero otherwise. 

Then the complete log-likelihood function becomes
\bea
l_c(\bm{\Psi}|\bm{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{j_d = 1}^{M_d} Z_{i,\bm{j}}\left[\log(\a_{j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_d}) + \sum_{k=1}^d \log\left(g(x_{ik};s^{(k)}_{j_k}, \bm{\theta}_k\right)\right].
\eea
The E-step of the EM algorithm computes $Q(\bm{\Psi};\bm{\Psi}^{old}) = \mE[l(\bm{\Psi}) |\bm{x}, \bm{\Psi}^{old}]$ where the expectation is taken with respect to the posterior probability of the membership indicator random variable $Z_{i,\bm{j}}$ given as
\bea
\a_{i,(j_1,\ldots,j_d)} : = P(Z_{i,\bm{j}} = 1 | \bm{x}_i, \bm{\Psi}^{old}) =  \frac{\a^{old}_{j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_d} \prod_{k=1}^d g(x_{ik};s^{(k)}_{j_k}, \bm{\theta}^{old}_k)}{\sum_{l_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{l_d = 1}^{M_d}\a^{old}_{l_1,l_2,\ldots,l_d} \prod_{k=1}^d g(x_{ik};s^{(k)}_{l_k}, \bm{\theta}^{old}_k)}.
\eea
The M-step of the EM algorithm consists of maximizing the following Q-function to obtain the updated parameter estimates 
\beq\label{Estep}\nonumber
Q(\bm{\Psi};\bm{x}, \bm{\Psi}^{old}) &=& \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{j_d = 1}^{M_d} \a_{i,(j_1,\ldots,j_d)}\left[\log \a_{j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_d} + \sum_{k=1}^d \log g(x_{ik};s^{(k)}_{j_k}, \bm{\theta}_k)\right]\\
&=& Q_1(\bm{\a};\bm{\Psi}^{old}) + Q_2(\bm{\theta}_1,\ldots, \bm{\theta}_d;\bm{\Psi}^{old}), 
\eeq
where
\bea
Q_1(\bm{\a};\bm{\Psi}^{old})  &=& \sum_{j_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{j_d = 1}^{M_d} \left(\sum_{i=1}^n \a_{i,(j_1,\ldots,j_d)}\right)\log \a_{j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_d}\\
Q_2(\bm{\theta}_1,\ldots, \bm{\theta}_d;\bm{\Psi}^{old}) &=& \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{j_d = 1}^{M_d} \a_{i,(j_1,\ldots,j_d)}\sum_{k=1}^d \log g(x_{ik};s^{(k)}_{j_k}, \bm{\theta}_k).
\eea

By the Lagrange multiplier we can obtain an explicit solution for the maximization of $Q_1$. For each $\bm{j} = (j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_d)$, the estimate of the mixture weight parameter is  
\bea
\hat{\a}_{\bm{j}} = \hat{\a}_{j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_d}  = \frac{1}{n}  \sum_{i=1}^n \a_{i,(j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_d)}.
\eea

The maximization of $Q_2$ depends on the specific form of the size-biased component density function $g$.  

In particular, the Erlang mixture component has the pdf, $g(x; s, \theta) = \theta^{-s} x^{s-1}e^{-x/\theta}/(s-1)!$. In this case we can obtain the following closed form expression for $\theta_k$:
\bea
\hat{\theta}_k = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n x_{ik}}{n \sum_{j_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{j_d = 1}^{M_d} \a_{i,(j_1,\ldots,j_k, \ldots, j_d)}s^{(k)}_{j_k}},\, k=1,\ldots, d.
%\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{j_d = 1}^{M_d} \a_{i,\bm{j}} \left(\sum_{k=1}^d x_{ik}\right)}{\sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{j_d = 1}^{M_d} \a_{i,\bm{j}} \left(\sum_{k=1}^d s^{(k)}_{jk}\right)}.
\eea

The size-biased Weibull (SBW) distribution extends the Erlang distribution with an additional shape parameter $\tau >0$ whose density function is given as 
$$
g(x;s, \tau, \theta) = \frac{\tau \theta^{-(\tau+ s -1)} x^{\tau + s -2}e^{-(x/\theta)^{\tau}}}{\Gamma[1+(s-1)/\tau]}.
$$
In this case the updated estimate for $\theta_k$ in the SBW mixture distribution is given as
\bea
\hat{\theta}_k = \left[\frac{\hat{\tau}_k \sum_{i=1}^n x^{\hat{\tau}_k}_{ik}}{n\left(\hat{\tau}_k-1 + \sum_{j_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{j_d = 1}^{M_d} \a_{i,(j_1,\ldots,j_d)} s^{(k)}_{jk}\right)}\right]^{1/\hat{\tau}_k},\, k = 1,\ldots, d,
\eea
where $\hat{\tau}_k$ is the solution of the following optimization problem:
\bea
\hat{\tau}_k = \argmax_{\tau_k} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{j_d = 1}^{M_d} \a_{i,(j_1,\ldots,j_d)}\log g(x_{ik};s^{(k)}_{j_k}, \tau_k,\hat{\theta}_k),\, k = 1,\ldots, d.
\eea
Due to the complexity of the objective function, a numerical method is required to solve the this problem.

The size-biased left-truncated lognormal distribution has the following the density function 
\bea
g(x;s, \sigma) = \frac{x^{s-2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\s^2}\left[\log(x/d)\right]^2\right\}}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi} d^{s-1}\exp\left\{(s-1)^2 \s^2 /2\right\}\Phi[(s-1)\sigma]},
\eea 
where $\Phi[\cdot]$ denotes the standard normal cdf. In this case the updated estimate for $\sigma_k$ in the SBltLN mixture distribution is the solution of the following equation:
\bea
\frac{d Q_2}{d \sigma_k} &=& - \frac{n}{\sigma_k} + \frac{1}{\sigma^3_k} \sum_{i=1}^n\left[\log (x_{ik}/d)\right]^2 - n \sigma_k \sum_{j_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{j_d = 1}^{M_d} \a_{i,(j_1,\ldots,j_d)} (s^{(k)}_{jk} -1)^2\\
&&\quad\quad - n\sum_{j_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{j_d = 1}^{M_d} \a_{i,(j_1,\ldots,j_d)} \frac{(s^{(k)}_{jk} -1)\phi[(s^{(k)}_{jk} -1)\sigma_k]}{\Phi[(s^{(k)}_{jk} -1)\sigma_k]} = 0,\,k=1,\ldots,d,
\eea
where $\phi[\cdot]$ denotes the standard normal density function.
 
\subsection{Penalized EM algorithm}
The performance of the estimated multivariate model largely depends on the number of components in the fitted mixture model. It is often observed that the fitted models based on the classical EM algorithm involve spurious or redundant components with near zero mixture weight estimates. Here we consider a penalized EM algorithm (PEM) to facilitate purging of statistically insignificant components, which results in a simpler model than the one fitted by the classical EM algorithm. See Figueriredo and Jain (2002)\nocite{Figueiredo} for details on the statistical learning of finite mixture models with various EM-based methods.
 
Specifically we aim to maximize the following penalized log-likelihood function 
\beq\label{eq:pl}
Pl(\bm{\Psi}|\bm{x}) = l(\bm{\Psi}|\bm{x}) - \gamma n R(\bm{\a}),
\eeq 
where $R(\bm{\a}) = \sum_{j_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{j_d = 1}^{M_d}\log \a_{j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_d}$ and $\gamma > 0$ is the tuning parameter that controls the degree of penalty. Clearly the penalty gets its minimum (or $-\infty$) when the mixture weights for some components become zero. That is, the model maximizing the penalized log-likelihood function tends to drop some statistically insignificant (or spurious) components. In fact, from a Bayesian perspective, Eq. (\ref{eq:pl}) can be obtained by assuming Dirichlet-type prior for $\bm{\a}$ and other model parameters are assumed constant. Specifically, we assume the prior density of $\bm{\a}$ as
\bea
p(\bm{\a}) \propto \prod_{j_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\prod_{j_d = 1}^{M_d}\left(\a_{j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_d}\right)^{-\gamma n }.
\eea
Then the logarithm of the posterior density (up to a constant) is given as (\ref{eq:pl}). 

With this specification, the PEM facilitates the mixture model to become parsimonious by encouraging some mixture weight parameter values to attain the value of zero. The PEM algorithm gives the following expectation step on the complete-data penalized log-likelihood function and the current parameter estimates 
\beq\label{rEstep}\nonumber
\tilde{Q}(\bm{\Psi};\bm{x}, \bm{\Psi}^{old}) &=& \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{j_d = 1}^{M_d} \a_{i,(j_1,\ldots,j_d)}\left[\log \a_{j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_d} + \sum_{k=1}^d \log g(x_{ik};s^{(k)}_{j_k}, \bm{\theta}_k)\right]\\\nonumber
&&\quad\quad\quad - \gamma n \sum_{j_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{j_d = 1}^{M_d}\log \a_{j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_d}\\\nonumber
&=& \tilde{Q}_1(\bm{\a};\bm{\Psi}^{old}) + \tilde{Q}_2(\bm{\theta}_1,\ldots, \bm{\theta}_d;\bm{\Psi}^{old}), 
\eeq
where
\bea
\tilde{Q}_1(\bm{\a};\bm{\Psi}^{old})  &=& \sum_{j_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{j_d = 1}^{M_d} \left(\sum_{i=1}^n (\a_{i,(j_1,\ldots,j_d)}-\gamma)\right) \log \a_{j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_d}\\
\tilde{Q}_2(\bm{\theta}_1,\ldots, \bm{\theta}_d;\bm{\Psi}^{old}) &=& \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{j_d = 1}^{M_d} \a_{i,(j_1,\ldots,j_d)}\sum_{k=1}^d \log g(x_{ik};s^{(k)}_{j_k}, \bm{\theta}_k).
\eea
With the constraints on the mixture weight parameters, $\a_{j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_d} \ge 0$ and $\sum_{j_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{j_d = 1}^{M_d} \a_{j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_d} = 1$, the method of Lagrange multiplier  renders an explicit solution for the maximization of $\tilde{Q}_1$. For each $\bm{j} = (j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_d)$, the updated estimate of the mixture weight parameter is given as
\bea
\hat{\a}^{R}_{j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_d}  = \frac{\max\{\sum_{i=1}^n \left(\a_{i,(j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_d)}-\gamma\right), 0\}}{\sum_{j_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{j_d = 1}^{M_d} \max\{\sum_{i=1}^n \left(\a_{i,(j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_d)}-\gamma\right), 0\}}.
\eea
That is, the estimate $\hat{\a}^{R}_{j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_d} = 0$ when $n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n \a_{i,(j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_d)} \le \gamma$. In other words, the higher the tuning parameter $\gamma$, the simpler becomes the fitted model. Note that $\tilde{Q}_2(\bm{\theta}_1,\ldots, \bm{\theta}_d;\bm{\Psi}^{old})$ is identical to $Q_2(\bm{\theta}_1,\ldots, \bm{\theta}_d;\bm{\Psi}^{old})$ in (\ref{Estep}), and thus the estimation of $\bm{\theta}_k$ can be proceeded as in Section \ref{em}.


\subsection{Parameter initialization}\label{initial}
The EM based estimation methods suffer from their sensitivity to the initial parameter values. Random sets of parameter values may be used repeatedly until get the optimal result. For high-dimensional case with a large number of components, the randomization method is computationally intense. The stochastic ordering structure and the weak-denseness property can help alleviate the computational burden in parameter initialization. 
Specifically for the univariate SBW mixture model, we proceed as follows. For each $\tau^{(0)}$,
\bea
\theta^{(0)} = \frac{X_{(n)}}{(M/\tau)^{1/\tau}},  \a_j^{(0)} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n I\left[\theta(s_{j-1}/\tau)^{1/\tau} < X_i \le \theta(s_j/\tau)^{1/\tau}\right]. 
\eea
Fit each marginal model using the EM (or PEM) algorithm. Use the estimated parameters for the initial values for the estimation of multivariate models. The weight parameters can be initialized as (Bivariate SBW mixture case for example)
\bea
\a_{l,m}^{(0)} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n I\left[\theta_1(s^{(1)}_{l-1}/\tau_1)^{1/\tau_1} < X_i \le \theta_1(s^{(1)}_l/\tau_1)^{1/\tau_1},	\right.\\
   \left.  \theta_2(s^{(2)}_{m-1}/\tau_2)^{1/\tau_2} < Y_{i} \le \theta_2(s^{(2)}_m/\tau_2)^{1/\tau_2}\right].
\eea

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Penalized EM algorithm}
For a fixed tuning parameter $\gamma$:
\begin{itemize}
\item [] \underline{Parameter initialization}
\item [] \textbf{While} the stopping criterion is not satisfied \textbf{do}\\
\underline{E-Step}\\
Calculate posterior probabilities for each $i$ and $j = (j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_d)$: 
$$
\a^{(r)}_{i,j} = \frac{\a^{(r-1)}_{j} \prod_{k=1}^d g(x_{ik};s^{(k)}_{j_k}, \bm{\theta}^{(r-1)}_k)}{\sum_{l_1 = 1}^{M_1} \cdots\sum_{l_d = 1}^{M_d}\a^{(r-1)}_{(l_1,l_2,\ldots,l_d)} \prod_{k=1}^d g(x_{ik};s^{(k)}_{l_k}, \bm{\theta}^{(r-1)}_k)}.
$$
Given the parameter estimates $\bm{\Psi}^{(r-1)} = (\bm{\a}^{(r-1)}, \bm{\Theta}^{(r-1)})$ obtained in the $(r-1)$th iteration, establish $\tilde{Q}$-function:
$$
\tilde{Q}(\bm{\Psi};\bm{\Psi}^{(r-1)}) = \tilde{Q}_1(\bm{\a};\bm{\Psi}^{(r-1)})  + \tilde{Q}_2(\bm{\Theta};\bm{\Psi}^{(r-1)}). 
$$
\underline{M-Step}
\bea
\bm{\a}^{(r)}\!\!\!\!\!&\leftarrow&\!\!\!\!\!  \argmax_{\bm{\a}} \tilde{Q}_1(\bm{\a};\bm{\Psi}^{(r-1)}): \bm{\a}^{(r)}_{j}\!=\! \frac{\max\{\sum_{i=1}^n \left(\a^{(r)}_{i,j}-\gamma\right), 0\}}{\sum_{j_1 = 1}^{M_1}\cdot\sum_{j_d = 1}^{M_d} \max\left\{\sum_{i=1}^n \left(\a^{(r)}_{i,(j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_d)}-\gamma\right), 0 \right\}}\\
\bm{\Theta}^{(r)}\!\!\!\!\!&\leftarrow&\!\!\!\!\! \argmax_{\bm{\Theta}} \tilde{Q}_2(\bm{\Theta};\bm{\Psi}^{(r-1)}).
\eea
Calculate the criterion e.g., $D \leftarrow |\bm{\Theta}^{(r)} - \bm{\Theta}^{(r-1)}|_1$ \\
$\bm{\Psi}^{(r)} \leftarrow (\bm{\a}^{(r)}, \bm{\Theta}^{(r)})$\\
\textbf{end}\\
\textbf{return} $\bm{\Psi} = (\bm{\a}, \bm{\Theta})$.
\end{itemize}
Use the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) to select the optimal value of $\gamma$ on the grid of possible values.
\end{algorithm}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec6}
In this paper, we have extended the univariate size-biased mixture framework to a multivariate setting, providing an effective tool for modeling complex, dependent loss data structures. By leveraging the weak-denseness property of SB mixtures, specifically Erlang, size-biased Weibull , and size-biased truncated lognormal mixture distributions, we demonstrated that these models can approximate a wide range of multivariate continuous distributions with high precision.

A key contribution of this research is the implementation of a Penalized EM (PEM) algorithm for parameter estimation. This approach effectively addresses the common issue of overfitting inherent in classical EM algorithms by purging statistically insignificant components. As demonstrated in our numerical examples, the PEM algorithm results in more parsimonious and interpretable models without sacrificing significant goodness-of-fit.

The application to a bivariate insurance liability dataset (Loss and ALAE) highlighted the practical utility of our proposed models. Our analysis revealed that while the Erlang mixture provides a flexible fit, it tends toward over-complexity with a high number of components. In contrast, the SBtLN mixture model offered the most efficient fit in terms of the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), while the SBW mixture proved effective in capturing the heavy-tailed dependence structures often found in insurance data. 

Overall, the multivariate size-biased mixture models, supported by penalized estimation, offers a powerful and flexible addition to the statistical toolkit for actuaries and data scientists working with heterogeneous, dependent populations.

Future research could explore the extension of this framework to include covariate effects through mixture-of-experts models or investigate the performance of these multivariate SB mixtures in higher-dimensional data. The lack of upper tail dependence poses a limitation of the proposed multivariate model. This issue is expected to be overcome by introducing shared latent model parameters. We will pursue this approach in the future.

\[
g(x;s,\theta) =\frac{x^{s-1}h(x;\theta)}{\mu(s-1;\theta)},
\]
we obtain, for every $r$ for which the moment exists,

\int_0^\infty x^r g(x;s,\theta),dx 
&=
\frac{1}{\mu(s-1;\theta)}
\int_0^\infty x^{r+s-1}h(x;\theta),dx\
&=

\end{aligned}

\textbf{Proposition 1}\\

For the bivariate density 
\[ f_{u,v}(x,y) =
\sum_{l=1}^{M_u}\sum_{m=1}^{M_v} \alpha^{(u,v)}_{lm} g(x;s_l^{(u)},\theta_u) g(y;s_m^{(v)},\theta_v), \] 
the covariance is 
\[ \operatorname{Cov}(X_u,X_v) =
\sum_{l=1}^{M_u}\sum_{m=1}^{M_v}
\left(
\alpha^{(u,v)}_{lm}
-\alpha_l^{(u)}\alpha_m^{(v)}
\right)
m(s_l^{(u)},\theta_u)m(s_m^{(v)},\theta_v).
\]

\textbf{Proof}\\

By the mixture representation and Tonelli’s theorem, 
\[
 \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[X_uX_v] &= \int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty xy,f_{u,v}(x,y),dx,dy\\ 
&= \sum_{l=1}^{M_u}\sum_{m=1}^{M_v} \alpha^{(u,v)}_{lm} \left[ \int_0^\infty xg(x;s_l^{(u)},\theta_u),dx \right] \left[ \int_0^\infty yg(y;s_m^{(v)},\theta_v),dy \right]. 
\end{aligned} 
\] 
Then  
\[ \mathbb E[X_uX_v] =
\sum_{l,m}
\alpha^{(u,v)}_{lm}
\frac{\mu(s_l^{(u)};\theta_u)}
     {\mu(s_l^{(u)}-1;\theta_u)}
\frac{\mu(s_m^{(v)};\theta_v)}
     {\mu(s_m^{(v)}-1;\theta_v)}.
\tag{A2}
\]
The marginal mixing probabilities are 
\[ \alpha_l^{(u)} =
\sum_m\alpha_{lm}^{(u,v)}, \qquad \alpha_m^{(v)}
\sum_l\alpha_{lm}^{(u,v)}. \] 
Therefore, 
\[ \mathbb E[X_u] =
\sum_l\alpha_l^{(u)}m(s_l^{(u)},\theta_u), \] and 
\[ \mathbb E[X_v] =
\sum_m\alpha_m^{(v)}m(s_m^{(v)},\theta_v).
\]
Consequently,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[X_u]\mathbb E[X_v]
&=
\sum_l\sum_m
\alpha_l^{(u)}\alpha_m^{(v)}
m(s_l^{(u)},\theta_u)m(s_m^{(v)},\theta_v).
\end{aligned}
\tag{A3}
\]
Subtracting (A3) from (A2) gives 
\[  \operatorname{Cov}(X_u,X_v) =
\sum_{l,m}
\left(
\alpha^{(u,v)}_{lm}
-\alpha_l^{(u)}\alpha_m^{(v)}
\right)
m(s_l^{(u)},\theta_u)m(s_m^{(v)},\theta_v).
\]

This proves Proposition 1. $\square$

\textbf{Proposition 2}\\

Let 
\[ F_u(x) = \sum_{l'=1}^{M_u}\alpha_{l'}^{(u)} G(x;s_{l'}^{(u)},\theta_u) \] and 
\[ F_v(y)=\sum_{m'=1}^{M_v}\alpha_{m'}^{(v)} G(y;s_{m'}^{(v)},\theta_v) \] be the marginal distribution functions. 
Then Spearman’s rho is 
\[ \rho =
12 \sum_{l,m,l',m'} \alpha^{(u,v)}_{lm} \alpha_{l'}^{(u)} \alpha_{m'}^{(v)} \lambda(s_l^{(u)},s_{l'}^{(u)},\theta_u) \lambda(s_m^{(v)},s_{m'}^{(v)},\theta_v) -3, \] where 
\[ \lambda(s,t,\theta)
\int_0^\infty G(x;s,\theta)g(x;t,\theta),dx.
\]

\textbf{Proof}\\
For continuous random variables, 
\[ U_u=F_u(X_u),\qquad U_v=F_v(X_v) 
\] 
are uniform on ($(0,1)$). Hence 
\[ \mathbb E[U_u]=\mathbb E[U_v]=\frac12, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(U_u)=\operatorname{Var}(U_v)=\frac1{12}, \] and thus 
\[ \rho =
12\mathbb E[F_u(X_u)F_v(X_v)]-3.
\tag{A4}
\]

Using the bivariate mixture density,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[F_u(X_u)F_v(X_v)]
&=
\sum_{l,m}\alpha^{(u,v)}_{lm}
\int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty
F_u(x)F_v(y) \times
g(x;s_l^{(u)},\theta_u)
g(y;s_m^{(v)},\theta_v)\,dx\,dy.
\end{aligned}
\]
Since the integrand separates in $x$ and $y$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[F_u(X_u)F_v(X_v)]
&=
\sum_{l,m}\alpha^{(u,v)}_{lm}
\left[
\int_0^\infty F_u(x)g(x;s_l^{(u)},\theta_u)\,dx
\right]\times
\left[
\int_0^\infty F_v(y)g(y;s_m^{(v)},\theta_v)\,dy
\right].
\end{aligned}
\tag{A5}
\]
Now expand the marginal cdfs: 
\[ F_u(x) =
\sum_{l'}\alpha_{l'}^{(u)} G(x;s_{l'}^{(u)},\theta_u). \] 
Therefore, 
\[ \begin{aligned} \int_0^\infty F_u(x)g(x;s_l^{(u)},\theta_u)\,dx &= \sum_{l'}\alpha_{l'}^{(u)} \int_0^\infty G(x;s_{l'}^{(u)},\theta_u) g(x;s_l^{(u)},\theta_u)\,dx. \end{aligned} \] 
Thus, 
\[ \int_0^\infty F_u(x)g(x;s_l^{(u)},\theta_u)\,dx = \sum_{l'}\alpha_{l'}^{(u)} \lambda(s_{l'}^{(u)},s_l^{(u)},\theta_u). \tag{A6} \] 
Similarly, 
\[ \int_0^\infty F_v(y)g(y;s_m^{(v)},\theta_v)\,dy = \sum_{m'}\alpha_{m'}^{(v)}\lambda(s_{m'}^{(v)},s_m^{(v)},\theta_v).
\tag{A7}
\]
Substituting (A6) and (A7) into (A5), and then into (A4), yields 
\[ \begin{aligned} \rho =
12 \sum_{l,m,l',m'} \alpha^{(u,v)}_{lm} \alpha{l'}^{(u)} \alpha_{m'}^{(v)} \lambda(s_{l'}^{(u)},s_l^{(u)},\theta_u) \lambda(s_{m'}^{(v)},s_m^{(v)},\theta_v) -3. \end{aligned} \] 
This proves Proposition 2. $\square$

\textbf{Proposition 3}\\

Kendall’s tau is 
\[ \tau =
4
\sum_{l,m,l',m'}
\alpha^{(u,v)}_{lm}
\alpha^{(u,v)}_{l'm'}
\lambda(s_l^{(u)},s_{l'}^{(u)},\theta_u)
\lambda(s_m^{(v)},s_{m'}^{(v)},\theta_v)
-1.
\]

\textbf{Proof}\\

Let
\[
(X_u,X_v) \quad \text{and}\quad (X_u',X_v')
\]
be independent and identically distributed copies of the bivariate size-biased mixture vector.
For continuous random variables, 
\[ \tau =
4\mathbb E\left[ F_{u,v}(X_u,X_v) \right]-1, \tag{A8} \] where 
\[ F_{u,v}(x,y) =
\Pr(X_u\le x,X_v\le y).
\]
The joint cdf is 
\[ F_{u,v}(x,y) =
\sum_{l',m'}
\alpha_{l'm'}^{(u,v)}
G(x;s_{l'}^{(u)},\theta_u)
G(y;s_{m'}^{(v)},\theta_v),
\tag{A9}
\]
because each component has independent coordinates.
Using the mixture representation of $(X_u,X_v)$, 
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[F_{u,v}(X_u,X_v)] &= \sum_{l,m}\alpha_{lm}^{(u,v)} \int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty F_{u,v}(x,y)\times g(x;s_l^{(u)},\theta_u) g(y;s_m^{(v)},\theta_v)\,dx\,dy. \end{aligned} \] 
Substitute (A9): 
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[F_{u,v}(X_u,X_v)] &= \sum_{l,m}\sum_{l',m'} \alpha_{lm}^{(u,v)}\alpha_{l'm'}^{(u,v)}\times \int_0^\infty G(x;s_{l'}^{(u)},\theta_u) g(x;s_l^{(u)},\theta_u)\,dx \times \int_0^\infty G(y;s_{m'}^{(v)},\theta_v) g(y;s_m^{(v)},\theta_v)\,dy. \end{aligned} \] 
By the definition of $\lambda$ function, 
\[ \mathbb E[F_{u,v}(X_u,X_v)] =\sum_{l,m,l',m'} \alpha_{lm}^{(u,v)}\alpha_{l'm'}^{(u,v)} \lambda(s_{l'}^{(u)},s_l^{(u)},\theta_u) \lambda(s_{m'}^{(v)},s_m^{(v)},\theta_v). \] 
Substitution into (A8) gives 
\[  \tau = 4
\sum_{l,m,l',m'}
\alpha_{lm}^{(u,v)}\alpha_{l'm'}^{(u,v)}
\lambda(s_l^{(u)},s_{l'}^{(u)},\theta_u)
\lambda(s_m^{(v)},s_{m'}^{(v)},\theta_v)
-1.
\]
Hence Proposition 3 follows. $\square$


\textbf{Proof of upper tail independence}

Let
\[
\bar G_{k,s}(x):=1-G(x;s,\theta_k)
=\int_x^\infty g(y;s,\theta_k),dy
\]
denote the survival function of a size-biased component in coordinate $k$, where
\[
g(y;s,\theta_k)=\frac{y^{s-1}h(y;\theta_k)}
{\mu(s-1;\theta_k)}.
\]

We prove that the upper-tail dependence coefficient
\[
\lambda_U
=\lim_{q\to1}
\Pr\left(X_u>F_u^{-1}(q)\mid X_v>F_v^{-1}(q)\right)
\]
is zero.

Set
\[
x_q=F_u^{-1}(q),\qquad y_q=F_v^{-1}(q).
\]
Since $F_u(x_q)=F_v(y_q)=q$,
\[
\Pr(X_u>x_q)=\Pr(X_v>y_q)=1-q.
\]
The joint survival function associated with (\ref{pair_density}) is 
\[ \Pr(X_u>x,X_v>y) = 
\sum_{l=1}^{M_u}\sum_{m=1}^{M_v} \alpha^{(u,v)}_{l,m} \bar G_{u,s_l^{(u)}}(x) \bar G_{v,s_m^{(v)}}(y), 
\] 
because the coordinates are conditionally independent within each mixture component. 

Hence 
\[ \lambda_U = 
\lim_{q\to1}
\frac{
\sum_{l,m}\alpha^{(u,v)}_{l,m}
\bar G_{u,s_l^{(u)}}(x_q)
\bar G_{v,s_m^{(v)}}(y_q)
}{1-q}.
\]
We now use the ordering of the size-biased components. If $s<t$, then 
\[ \bar G_{k,s}(x) =
\frac{1}{\mu(s-1;\theta_k)}
\int_x^\infty z^{s-1}h(z;\theta_k),dz
\]
and, for $z\ge x$,
\[
z^{s-1}
=z^{t-1}z^{s-t}
\le x^{s-t}z^{t-1}.
\]
Therefore,
\[
\bar G_{k,s}(x)
\le
\frac{\mu(t-1;\theta_k)}
{\mu(s-1;\theta_k)}
x^{s-t}\bar G_{k,t}(x).
\]
Since $s-t<0$, it follows that
\[
\frac{\bar G_{k,s}(x)}{\bar G_{k,t}(x)}
\longrightarrow 0
\qquad (x\to\infty).
\]
Thus, among the finitely many size-biased components, the component with the largest order dominates the upper tail. In particular, for every $(l,m)$, there are finite constants $(C_{u,l},C_{v,m})$ such that, for sufficiently large $(x,y)$,
\[
\bar G_{u,s_l^{(u)}}(x)\le C_{u,l}\bar F_u(x),
\qquad
\bar G_{v,s_m^{(v)}}(y)\le C_{v,m}\bar F_v(y),
\]
where
\[
\bar F_u(x)=1-F_u(x),\qquad
\bar F_v(y)=1-F_v(y).
\]

Consequently,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\Pr(X_u>x_q,X_v>y_q)
&\le
\sum_{l,m}\alpha^{(u,v)}_{l,m}
C_{u,l}C_{v,m}
\bar F_u(x_q)\bar F_v(y_q)\
&=C(1-q)^2
\end{aligned}
\]
for some finite constant $C$. Dividing by $\Pr(X_v>y_q)= 1-q$ gives
\[
\Pr(X_u>x_q\mid X_v>y_q)
\le C(1-q)\longrightarrow 0.
\]

Therefore,  $\lambda_U=0$. Hence any pair of random variables from the bivariate size-biased mixture is asymptotically independent in the upper tail even though dependence can still arise at finite levels through the joint mixture weights $\alpha^{(u,v)}_{l,m}$.
